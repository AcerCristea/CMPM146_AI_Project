The Aldermere Ledger

A game economy where prices are state, producers adapt with a genetic algorithm, and a year of ordinary adventuring leaves a measurable mark on the county — measured against the same year with the player removed.

Most game economies are lookup tables. This one is a ledger of everything the player did, most of which they were not thinking about economically.

The problem

A sword costs 150 gold on day one and on day four hundred, whatever happened in between. Games that do move prices usually do it with a scripted multiplier: a quest flag sets iron to ×1.3 for a week. The player learns quickly that the economy is a prop.

The goal here was an economy that moves for reasons — where a blight, a discovery, or a player burning four acres of wheat on a botched spell all reach the price through the same physical mechanism, and where the consequences of many small acts accumulate into something the player can eventually see.

The system

Three layers, each usable without the ones above it. Pure Python, no dependencies.

Market engine

One town. Price is persistent state that chases excess demand with inertia and rails. Households buy by need priority with per-good elasticity. Producers are a population whose genome is a land allocation; a GA breeds the profitable ones every three days, so the economy re-equilibrates after every shock instead of being solved once.

economy.py

Trade layer

Several towns with different land quality, coupled only by NPC merchants who buy where a good is cheap and haul it where it is dear. Price convergence, shortages spreading between towns, and a merchant class that keeps a measurable cut are all emergent.

trade.py

Game layer

A player who takes work, hears rumours instead of reading a debug panel, and leaves a footprint on the county with every outing. At the end the year is replayed without them.

How a day works

production   land share × land × yield × efficiency          (events change efficiency)
demand       population × need × (base price / price)^elasticity
clearing     buy by priority until the budget runs out; unsold stock spoils
price        price × (1 + 0.18 × excess demand), 55% inertia, clamped 0.2–5× base
fitness      produced × price × sell-through, smoothed   (goods nobody bought earn nothing)
evolve       every 3 days: elites survive, tournament crossover, gaussian mutation in logit space

Two of those lines carry most of the weight. Elasticity is what separates a necessity from a luxury: wheat at 0.35 barely loses demand when its price doubles, tools at 1.6 lose two-thirds. And sell-through in the fitness is what prevents monoculture — flooding a market punishes you, so producers diversify on their own.

Events move prices, and the market recovers

The signature curve. A blight cuts grain yield to 30% on day 60 and recovers linearly. Wheat roughly doubles, farmers shift land into grain, supply returns, price settles. Nobody scripted the shape.

Merchants integrate the towns

Three towns — Riverfold farms, Loomhaven weaves, Ashmoor mines — run in isolation drift apart. Merchants exist to exploit the gap. The test is the same world, same seed, with the fleet deleted.

An accounting check that was not planned. Over one run the towns received 159,985 for exports and paid 250,489 for imports. The gap is 90,503. Total merchant profit is 90,503. Money is conserved through the trade layer to the gold.

Getting here took three passes, each of which found a real pathology. Merchants could only buy leftover stock, but an equilibrium market clears to nothing — a deadlock, fixed by letting them bid before the market opens so an unfilled order still moves the price. Then they ping-ponged mithril between two towns that mined none, each trade moving a thin price enough to fund the next — a pump, fixed by only buying at the source. Then bids ten times a town's output swamped every price signal and all three towns planted wheat — fixed by capping export bids against supply.

The year without you

This is the part worth pitching. The player takes 72 outings in a year — goblins in a mine shaft, wasps in the cotton, a hedge mage who wants a fire ward tested. Each leaves a footprint of a few percent on a yield for a few weeks, below the market's own noise. Some go wrong: the fire ward holds 60% of the time.

At the end, the whole year is replayed with those footprints removed. The difference is attributable to the player and nothing else.

Where it came from

The report attributes pressure to outings. A player can read that the shaft they shored in week four is why iron is where it is.

Validation. A player who takes no outings at all is bit-identical to the counterfactual ghost on every price and on prosperity, and the ghost is deterministic across repeated runs. The “without you” column is a true control, not an estimate.

What accumulates — and what does not

The original pitch was that small price effects would add up over a long game. Measured, they do not:

Price difference peaks and reverts. The reason is the GA doing its job: shift the supply of something and producers move land until revenue per acre is level again. Temporary supply shocks leave no permanent price mark in a market with adaptive producers — which is correct economics, and it quietly killed the naive version of the pitch.

So the claim that survives measurement is sharper than the one that went in: the county's productive capacity is a ledger of everything the player did. Prices are the visible surface and they mostly recover. The durable change is structural, and it is what a game should show.

Work pays better in a town that eats better — late-year wages of 1.27× against 1.12× — and mithril kit exists because they found the ore, but they decline the cattle raid's plunder and that shows in the purse. Doing right is competitive, not free. That seemed like the more honest game.

What did not work

  • “Cheaper gear in a prosperous town” as a reward. More ore makes tools cheaper, households buy more, producers shift land away, the price recovers. The runs that improved ore land most did not have cheaper iron. Wages follow prosperity instead, because prosperity is the thing that actually accumulates.
  • Reputation gating work. Built, measured, worked — and cut. It is Fable's mechanic, and the point of this project is consequence the player was not looking for, not a morality meter.
  • A hunger loop. The adventurer buys bread weekly, so wrecking the grain supply should cost them. It cannot: one person eats fourteen measures a week and a granary raid pays seven hundred gold. The arithmetic never favours virtue at that scale.
  • Destroying stock. A market that clears daily holds about half a day of cloth on the shelf, so “burn 400 cloth” burned 36 and moved the price 2%. Adding stock works; taking it away cannot exceed what exists. The warehouse fire became a supply disruption to the weavers instead.
  • Tuning. Every fix above revealed the next pathology. That is the nature of emergent systems, and the honest answer to a studio asking “how long to tune this” is: longer than you think. The diagnostic report is the best answer to that question.

What comes next

  • Seasons. Harvest cycles are the single biggest missing piece for a medieval feel.
  • Durables. Cloth and tools bought rarely and worn out, so they stop being 27% of a daily budget.
  • Imperfect information for merchants, so they speculate and sometimes lose.
  • Population that responds to being fed.
  • Save/load, a cost-per-tick budget, and a clean API: tick(), price_of(good, town), trigger(event), player_trade().
